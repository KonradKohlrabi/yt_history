**THE AMERICAN CIVIL WAR: A HOUSE DIVIDED**

**(Opening on black. The sound of a single train whistle, low and mournful, cutting through silence. Then, the rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of a locomotive building speed.)**

---

### I. STAKES: THE EXPERIMENT ON TRIAL

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
In the spring of 1861, the United States was not yet a nation in the singular sense. It was a fragile coalition of opposites. Twenty-two million people in the North—industrial, immigrant-fueled, tied by iron rails and a "free labor" ideology. Nine million in the South—four million of them human beings owned as property, their labor worth three billion dollars, the largest financial asset in the entire country.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The Constitution had papered over the crack with compromises. Three-fifths of a person. A fugitive slave clause. A ban on importation that only drove the internal trade deeper. For seventy years, they had kicked the can down the road. But the road had run out.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
On November 6, 1860, a tall, gangly lawyer from Illinois won the presidency with thirty-nine percent of the vote. He carried not a single Southern state. His name was Abraham Lincoln. His platform: slavery would not spread one inch further.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
In Charleston, South Carolina, they didn't wait for his inauguration. On December 20th, they voted 169 to 0 to dissolve the Union. Six more states followed. They formed a new government in Montgomery, Alabama. Their Vice President, Alexander Stephens, didn't mince words. The Confederacy’s cornerstone, he declared, rested upon "the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Four million souls in chains. The survival of the world’s only large-scale democracy. The question of whether a government "of the people, by the people, for the people" could long endure.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
This is the story of how that question was answered—in blood, in fire, and in a transformation no one saw coming.

---

### II. THE BIG QUESTION: ONE WAR AT A TIME

**(Visual: The unfinished Capitol Dome in Washington. Cranes frozen against the sky. Lincoln taking the oath, hand on a borrowed Bible.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lincoln arrived in Washington secretly, disguised, smuggled through Baltimore in the night. Assassins waited in the shadows. He swore to "preserve, protect, and defend." But the Confederacy had already seized federal arsenals, mints, and forts. Only four forts in the Deep South still flew the Stars and Stripes.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The biggest sat in the mouth of Charleston Harbor: Fort Sumter. Major Robert Anderson and eighty-five men. Food running out. Lincoln faced a choice: evacuate and legitimize the Confederacy, or resupply and risk war.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
He chose a third path. He told the South: *I am sending food. Only food. If you fire on bread, you start the war.*

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
On April 12, 1861, at 4:30 a.m., a mortar shell arced over the harbor. It burst above Sumter. A signal. Forty-three guns opened up. For thirty-four hours, the fort burned. Anderson surrendered. The only casualty: a horse. And one man, Daniel Hough, killed accidentally during a hundred-gun salute to the lowering flag.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers. Ninety days. The North erupted in patriotic fervor. "On to Richmond!" the newspapers screamed. The South rejoiced. They believed one Southerner equaled ten Yankees.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
But four slave states refused to leave—Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri, Delaware. Lincoln stared at a map. "I hope to have God on my side," he said, "but I must have Kentucky."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The Big Question hung over the White House that summer: *Can this rebellion be crushed in a single campaign? Or has the Union already fractured beyond repair?*

---

### III. HEAD FAKE #1: THE ILLUSION OF GLORY

**(Visual: July 21, 1861. Manassas Junction, Virginia. Picnickers from Washington with opera glasses and champagne baskets on the hills.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
They came to watch the war end. Senators. Clerks. Women in bonnets. General Irvin McDowell’s green army marched toward Bull Run Creek, confident, singing.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
For a few hours, it looked like a parade. The Union flank swept the Confederates back. Then, on Henry House Hill, a brigade under Thomas Jackson stood immobile. "There stands Jackson like a stone wall!" Bee shouted. *Stonewall.*

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The Confederate counterattack didn't just push the Union back. It shattered them. The retreat became a rout. The picnickers fled, carriages blocking the roads, Congressmen trying to rally panicked soldiers with umbrellas.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Nearly 5,000 casualties. More than all previous American wars combined. The ninety-day war was a lie.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Head Fake:* The South thought they had won independence. The North thought they had lost the war.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Reality:* Both sides realized this would be a war of armies, not a skirmish. In Washington, a young engineer named George McClellan was handed the reins. He would build an army. But he would not use it.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Rehook:* While the East froze in stalemate, the war moved West. Where a quiet, failed clerk named Ulysses S. Grant was about to change the map.

---

### IV. THE WESTERN THEATER: UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER

**(Visual: February 1862. Ironclads churning brown water. Fort Henry drowning in flood. Fort Donelson surrounded by ice and blue coats.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Grant didn't maneuver for glory. He moved for rivers. The Tennessee. The Cumberland. The highways into the Confederate heartland.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Fort Henry fell before the infantry arrived—the river did the work. At Donelson, the Confederates tried to break out. They almost did. But Grant, calm amid chaos, ordered a counterattack. "No terms except unconditional and immediate surrender."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Twelve thousand Confederates stacked arms. The Cumberland River was open. Nashville fell—the first Confederate capital captured. Grant became a legend overnight. "Unconditional Surrender" Grant.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
But the Confederacy struck back. At Shiloh, April 1862. Albert Sidney Johnston threw 40,000 men at Grant’s unsuspecting camps. The Union line bent, broke, reformed in a sunken road—the "Hornet’s Nest." Johnston bled to death from a severed artery, a tourniquet never applied. Night fell. Rain poured. Grant sat under a tree, soaked, smoking a cigar. Sherman approached. "Well, Grant, we've had the devil's own day." Grant: "Yes. Lick 'em tomorrow, though."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Reinforcements arrived. Grant attacked. The Confederates fled. Twenty-three thousand casualties. More than the Revolution, the War of 1812, and the Mexican War *combined*. The scale had shifted. There was no going back.

---

### V. HEAD FAKE #2: THE GENERAL WHO WOULDN'T FIGHT

**(Visual: Spring 1862. The Peninsula. McClellan’s massive army—100,000 strong—creeping up the Virginia coast. Balloons rising for reconnaissance. The unfinished Capitol dome in the distance.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
McClellan had the men, the guns, the supplies. He had the "Young Napoleon" reputation. He also had the "slows." He believed he was outnumbered two to one. He was wrong by a factor of two.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
He besieged Yorktown for a month against a token force. He crept toward Richmond. Then Robert E. Lee took command of the Army of Northern Virginia.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lee didn't defend. He attacked. Seven Days Battles. Mechanicsville. Gaines' Mill. Glendale. Malvern Hill. Lee lost tactically—horrific casualties attacking entrenched positions—but strategically, he unhinged McClellan. The "Young Napoleon" retreated to the James River, abandoning the Peninsula. Richmond was saved.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Head Fake:* The South celebrated Lee as a savior. The North despaired.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Reality:* Lee’s aggression bled his army white. And in Washington, Lincoln made a decision that changed the war’s soul.

---

### VI. THE CROSSROADS: ANTIETAM & EMANCIPATION

**(Visual: September 17, 1862. A cornfield in Maryland. Morning mist. The "Lost Order"—Special Order 191—wrapped around three cigars, found in a field by a Union corporal.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lee invaded Maryland. He wanted a victory on Northern soil to trigger European recognition. He divided his army. A copy of his orders, wrapped around cigars, was lost in a field. A Union soldier found it. "Here is a paper with which if I cannot whip Bobbie Lee, I will be willing to go home," McClellan telegraphed.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
He waited eighteen hours.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
At dawn, the Cornfield erupted. Men fell in rows, scythed down. The Sunken Road—Bloody Lane—filled with bodies stacked three deep. Burnside’s Bridge became a slaughter pen. A.P. Hill’s Light Division arrived at the last moment, marching seventeen miles in seven hours, saving Lee’s right flank.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Twenty-two thousand casualties. One day. The bloodiest single day in American history.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lee retreated. McClellan had him. He had fresh reserves. He did nothing. "McClellan has the slows," Lincoln muttered.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
But it was enough. A tactical draw. A strategic Union victory. Lincoln went to his desk. He had drafted a proclamation months earlier. Seward had warned: *Wait for a victory, or it looks like a cry of despair.*

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Antietam was the victory. On September 22, 1862, Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. On January 1, 1863, it took effect.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
It freed no one immediately—exempting border states, occupied zones. But it reframed the war. It made the destruction of slavery a Union war aim. It authorized Black enlistment. And in London and Paris, the working classes—starving from the cotton famine—cheered. Their governments dared not intervene.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Rehook:* The war had a new purpose. But the killing had only just begun.

---

### VII. THE HIGH WATER MARK: GETTYSBURG & VICKSBURG

**(Visual: Summer 1863. Two theaters. One week.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lee marched north again. Pennsylvania. He needed shoes, supplies, a peace movement in the North. He stumbled into Gettysburg.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
July 1. Buford’s cavalry holds the high ground. Reynolds falls. The Union retreats through town to Cemetery Hill. Ewell hesitates. The high ground is lost.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
July 2. Longstreet attacks late. Sickles moves forward without orders—Peach Orchard, Wheatfield, Devil’s Den. On Little Round Top, the 20th Maine runs out of ammunition. Chamberlain orders a bayonet charge down the slope. They sweep the Alabamians away.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
July 3. The grand gamble. Pickett’s Charge. 12,500 men across three-quarters of a mile of open field. The artillery barrage overshoots. Union rifles and canister tear holes in the lines. Armistead breaches the wall—mortally wounded. The Confederacy’s high water mark recedes.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Fifty-one thousand casualties. Lee retreats. Meade follows... slowly. The rain swells the Potomac. Lee escapes.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Simultaneously, 1,000 miles away:* Grant sits before Vicksburg. The "Gibraltar of the Mississippi." He has marched his army down the Louisiana shore, crossed at Bruinsburg, won five battles in three weeks, invested the city.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Forty-seven days of siege. Civilians in caves. Rats eaten. Mules eaten. On July 4th—Pemberton surrenders. 29,000 men paroled. The Mississippi flows "unvexed to the sea." The Confederacy is bisected.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Head Fake:* The North celebrates Gettysburg as *the* turning point.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Reality:* Vicksburg was the strategic masterstroke. Grant. The man who fights.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Rehook:* Lincoln promotes Grant. General-in-Chief. All armies. One strategy. Simultaneous pressure. No more trading space for time.

---

### VIII. THE GRIND: 1864 – THE YEAR OF DECISION

**(Visual: The Wilderness. Dense, second-growth forest. Orange flashes in the gloom. Fire consuming the wounded.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
May 1864. Grant crosses the Rapidan. He doesn't want Richmond. He wants Lee’s army.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The Wilderness. Two days. Thirty thousand casualties. The woods catch fire. Wounded men burn alive. Grant receives a report of disaster. He doesn't retreat north. He turns *south* toward Spotsylvania. The men cheer. They know: *This man will not turn back.*

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Spotsylvania. The "Bloody Angle." Twenty hours of hand-to-hand combat in pouring rain. Men shoot through logs. The dead pile four deep. The oak tree twenty-two inches thick is cut down *by musket balls alone.*

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Cold Harbor. June 3. Seven thousand Union casualties in thirty minutes. Pinned by paperwork on their backs—name, regiment—so their bodies can be identified. Grant later calls it his greatest regret.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
But Lee cannot replace his losses. Grant can.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Grant crosses the James River—a engineering marvel—and hits Petersburg. The rail hub. Lee is pinned. Nine months of trenches. The preview of the Western Front, 1914.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
*Rehook:* The war hinges on November. The election. If Lincoln loses, the peace platform wins. The Union dissolves.

---

### IX. TOTAL WAR: ATLANTA & THE MARCH

**(Visual: September 2, 1864. Atlanta burning. Sherman’s telegram: "Atlanta is ours, and fairly won.")**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Sherman took Atlanta. Lincoln won. 55 percent. 212 electoral votes. The soldiers voted 78 percent for Lincoln. "Don't change horses mid-stream."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Then Sherman cut loose. Sixty-two thousand men. No supply lines. "Make Georgia howl."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Three hundred miles to Savannah. Rails heated, twisted around trees—"Sherman's neckties." Barns, mills, cotton gins burned. Not wanton cruelty—calculated destruction of the *capacity* to wage war. "War is cruelty," Sherman wrote. "The crueler it is, the sooner it will be over."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
He offered Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift. Then turned north. Columbia, South Carolina—the cradle of secession—burned. The war came home.

---

### X. THE END & THE BEGINNING: APPOMATTOX

**(Visual: April 1865. Petersburg lines break. Richmond evacuated. The Confederate government fleeing on trains. The city burning—arson, wind, chaos.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
April 2. The line snaps. Lee abandons Petersburg. Richmond burns. Davis flees.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
April 9. Appomattox Court House. Lee, immaculate in dress uniform. Grant, mud-spattered private’s coat. They meet in Wilmer McLean’s parlor—the same man whose farm hosted the first battle at Bull Run. The war started in his front yard. It ended in his front room.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Terms: Generous. Officers keep sidearms. Men keep horses—spring planting. Rations for the starving Confederates. "The rebels are our countrymen again," Grant orders salutes stopped.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Lee rides back through his lines. Men weep. He removes his hat. "Men, we have fought through the war together. I have done the best I could for you."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
It was over. But not finished.

---

### XI. THE UNFINISHED REVOLUTION

**(Visual: April 14, 1865. Ford’s Theatre. A single shot. The box flag draped in black.)**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Five days later. Good Friday. John Wilkes Booth. *Sic semper tyrannis.* Lincoln dies at 7:22 a.m. "Now he belongs to the ages."

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The martyrdom sealed the myth. But the work remained.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The 13th Amendment. Ratified December 1865. Slavery abolished.
The 14th. Citizenship. Equal protection. Due process.
The 15th. The vote.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Reconstruction. The Freedmen’s Bureau. Black senators. Black congressmen. Schools. Churches. Land.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Then the retreat. 1877. Federal troops withdrawn. The "Redemption." Jim Crow. Lynching. The Lost Cause myth—monuments rising not in 1865, but in 1910, 1920, 1960. A war over memory.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
Seven hundred fifty thousand dead. Two and a half percent of the population. Equivalent to seven million today.

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The Union survived. Slavery died. But the promise of equality—*that* war is still being fought.

**(Visual: The Lincoln Memorial. The Gettysburg Address carved in stone. "Government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth.")**

**NARRATOR (V.O.)**
The question Lincoln asked at Gettysburg remains the question of every generation since.

**(Fade to black.)**
