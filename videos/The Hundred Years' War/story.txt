# THE WAR THAT MADE TWO NATIONS
## A Documentary Script

---

### PROLOGUE: THE FEUDAL TRAP

**1328. The Cathedral of Reims.**

The crown of Charlemagne rests on the head of Philip of Valois. Around him, the great lords of France kneel. But one chair remains empty. The King of England—Edward III, grandson of a French king, nephew of the dead Charles IV—has not come to kneel.

He cannot. To kneel for his French lands—Gascony, the rich wine country of the southwest—is to admit he is a subject. But to refuse is treason.

*This is the trap. A king who is a vassal. A vassal who is a king.*

**[STAKES]**

Two kingdoms. One bloodline. A feudal contradiction that has festered for a century.

**Who is affected?** Every peasant in Flanders who spins English wool into cloth. Every vintner in Bordeaux who ships wine to London. Every archer in Cheshire, every knight in Normandy. The Kings of England and France, yes—but also the Dukes of Burgundy, the Counts of Flanders, the Scots, the Castilians, the Holy Roman Emperor, and two rival Popes.

**What is at stake?** Not just a duchy. The very shape of Western Europe. Will England become a continental empire, straddling the Channel? Or will France consolidate into the first true nation-state?

**Why is this extraordinary?** Because the war hasn't started yet. And it will not end for **116 years**—a span so vast that the men who fire the first arrows will have great-grandsons who witness the last cannon shot. The name "Hundred Years' War" is a lie coined by historians centuries later. The participants simply called it *la guerre de cent ans*... or just *la guerre*. The war.

**What if they fail?** If England loses: the end of the Plantagenet dream, a retreat to an island, civil war. If France loses: the extinction of the Valois line, the erasure of France as a sovereign entity, ruled from London by an English king.

---

### ACT I: THE CLAIM (1337–1360)

**[BIG QUESTION]**

*Can a younger son of a French princess wear the crown of France?*

**1337. Paris. The Great Council.**

Philip VI pronounces the sentence: *The Duchy of Aquitaine is confiscated.* The King of England has refused liege homage. He has harbored a French rebel, Robert of Artois. The fief reverts to the Crown.

Edward III receives the news in Westminster. He does not invade. Not yet. He builds a coalition. He buys the Emperor. He woos the Flemish cities—Ghent, Bruges, Ypres—whose looms starve without English wool. In Ghent, a brewer named **Jacob van Artevelde** convinces the burghers to recognize Edward as *King of France*.

**1340. The Estuary of the Zwin. Sluys.**

Two hundred ships. The French, Castilian, and Genoese fleet lies chained together, a floating fortress. Edward stands on the *Cog Thomas*, his bowmen in the high castles raining arrows. The Genoese crossbowmen—strings soaked by rain—cannot answer. By nightfall, the French admirals are dead. The French navy ceases to exist.

Edward besieges Tournai. He runs out of money. The Bardi and Peruzzi bankers of Florence collapse under his debts. He returns to England, bankrupt but master of the Channel.

**1346. The Chevauchée.**

Edward lands in Normandy. He burns. He loots. He marches to the gates of Paris, then turns north, chased by a massive French army. At the Somme, he traps himself. He crosses at Blanchetaque—low tide, desperate fight—and reaches a ridge near **Crécy-en-Ponthieu**.

**August 26, 1346. Crécy.**

The French knights charge. Sixteen times. Into mud. Into stakes. Into a storm of arrows from seven thousand longbows. The King of Bohemia—blind, tied to his knights—rides to his death. The Count of Flanders dies. The flower of French chivalry bleeds out in the dirt.

Edward’s son, the **Black Prince**, sixteen years old, commands the right wing. "Let the boy win his spurs," the King says. The boy does.

**Calais. Eleven months of starvation.**

The burghers eat dogs, cats, leather. Six leaders walk out with ropes around their necks, keys in hand. Edward’s queen, Philippa, heavy with child, begs for their lives. They live. Calais becomes an English town for two centuries. The "brightest jewel in the Crown."

**1348. The Black Death.**

The war stops. Not by treaty. By plague. Half of Europe dies. The longbowmen die. The knights die. The peasants die. Wages soar. The Statute of Laborers tries to freeze them. It fails.

**1356. Poitiers.**

The Black Prince, now a man, raids north from Bordeaux. King John II—the Good—corners him south of Poitiers. The French dismount. They climb a hedge under arrow-fire. They die exhausted. The King fights with a battle-axe, surrounded. He surrenders to a French knight in English service: *Denis de Morbecque.*

The King of France is a prisoner in London.

**[HEAD FAKE]**

*It looks over. England has won. Two battles. A king captive. A third of France ceded in the Treaty of Brétigny (1360). Full sovereignty over Aquitaine. Calais. Ponthieu. Three million écus ransom. Edward III renounces the French crown.*

*Peace. Real peace. The war is done.*

---

### ACT II: THE WISE KING & THE CONSTABLE (1360–1389)

**[REHOOK]**

*But the treaty is a trap. The sovereignty clause is ambiguous. The ransom is unpayable. And in Paris, a new King has learned the lesson of Crécy and Poitiers.*

**Charles V. The Wise. The Dauphin who ruled while his father rotted in the Tower.**

He does not fight battles. He builds a state. He reforms the coinage. He creates the *Chambre des Comptes*. He makes the *taille* permanent—taxation without the Estates General. He builds the Bastille. He fills the Louvre with books.

And he finds a soldier: **Bertrand du Guesclin**. A Breton squire. Ugly. Brilliant. He fights dirty. He avoids the English archers. He burns their supplies. He starves their garrisons. He takes castles by trickery—disguised as woodcutters, as merchants.

**1369. The Appeal.**

The Black Prince, ruling Aquitaine from Bordeaux, imposes a hearth tax. The Gascon lords appeal to the *Parlement of Paris*—their feudal superior. Charles V accepts the appeal. He declares the Prince contumacious. He confiscates Aquitaine.

War resumes.

**1372. La Rochelle.**

The English fleet enters the harbor. The Castilian galleys—low, oared, armed with Greek fire—wait. The tide turns. The English cogs ground. The Castilians swarm. The Earl of Pembroke surrenders. The sea lanes are lost. Aquitaine is cut off.

Du Guesclin rolls up the English positions. Poitiers. Bergerac. La Réole. By 1375, only Bordeaux, Bayonne, Brest, Cherbourg, and Calais remain.

**1376. The Black Prince dies.** A long, wasting illness. His son, Richard, is nine.

**1377. Edward III dies.** The great King, the founder of the Order of the Garter, the victor of Crécy, dies of a stroke, attended by a rapacious mistress who strips the rings from his fingers.

**1380. Du Guesclin dies** besieging a minor castle in Auvergne. Charles V follows him to the grave weeks later.

**1389. Leulinghem.**

A truce. Three years. Extended to twenty-eight. The guns fall silent. A generation grows up knowing peace.

---

### ACT III: THE MAD KING & THE CIVIL WAR (1389–1415)

**[REHOOK]**

*But in Paris, the King goes mad.*

**1392. The Forest of Le Mans.**

Charles VI rides with his army. A leper rushes from the trees: *"King, you are betrayed!"* A page drops a lance. The clang snaps the King’s mind. He slaughters his own knights. He falls into a coma. He wakes... but he is never whole again. He forgets his name. He forgets he is King. He believes he is made of glass.

**The Vacuum.**

Two uncles fight for control. **Louis of Orléans**, the King’s brother—charming, corrupt, the Queen’s lover. **John the Fearless**, Duke of Burgundy—ruthless, popular, master of the Low Countries.

**1407. The Rue Vieille du Temple.**

Louis leaves the Queen’s chambers. Masked assassins—Burgundian men-at-arms—cut him down. John the Fearless admits it. *Tyrannicide*, his theologian Jean Petit argues. The King was a tyrant. The Duke did France a service.

Civil War. **Armagnacs** (Orléans faction) vs. **Burgundians**. Paris massacres. The Cabochiens butcher Armagnacs in the streets. The Armagnacs retake Paris, purge Burgundians.

**Henry IV of England** watches. He cannot intervene—he has Welsh rebels (Glyndŵr), Scottish raids, a shaky throne. But his son...

---

### ACT IV: THE LION OF ENGLAND (1415–1422)

**[BIG QUESTION]**

*Can a usurper’s son conquer a kingdom with 6,000 men?*

**1415. Henry V.** Twenty-eight. Cold. Pious. Ruthless. He has crushed the Lollards. He has executed his friend Lord Scrope for treason. He lands at **Harfleur** with 12,000 men.

Dysentery takes half. The siege drags. The town falls. Henry marches for Calais—*a chevauchée to show the flag*. The French army shadows him. The Somme blocks the way. He crosses at Béthencourt—another low-tide miracle.

**October 25, 1415. Agincourt. St. Crispin’s Day.**

A narrow field. Freshly plowed. Rain all night. Mud knee-deep.

Henry’s men—6,000, mostly archers, starving, sick—plant their stakes. The French—12,000 to 25,000, the flower of a new generation—refuse to attack. They wait for more men. Henry advances. *Within arrow range.*

He orders: *"Banners advance."*

The archers loose. The French charge. The mud sucks them down. The stakes break the momentum. The archers drop bows, grab hatchets, mallets, knives. They slaughter the nobility in the mud. The Constable d’Albret. The Dukes of Alençon, Brabant, Bar. Ninety great lords dead. Fifteen hundred prisoners.

Henry orders the prisoners killed—fear of a rear attack. The nobles refuse. He sends archers to do it.

**[FUN FACT INTEGRATION]**

*Years later, at Patay, the French will learn. They will charge before the stakes are set. But here, at Agincourt, the mud is England’s ally.*

**1417–1419. The Conquest of Normandy.**

Henry does not raid. He conquers. Town by town. Caen. Cherbourg. Rouen—six months of starvation. Women and children expelled into the ditches between the walls. Henry watches them die. He takes the city. He makes it English.

**1419. The Bridge at Montereau.**

The Dauphin Charles—eighteen, hardened by Armagnac intrigue—meets John the Fearless on a bridge. A parley. The Dauphin’s men kill the Duke. An axe to the skull. Just like Louis of Orléans, twelve years before.

**Philip the Good**, the new Duke of Burgundy, turns to England.

**1420. Troyes.**

The Treaty. Charles VI—mad, manipulated—disinherits his own son. Henry V marries Catherine. He is Heir and Regent of France. The Dual Monarchy. One King, two crowns, separate laws.

**1422. The Year of Two Deaths.**

August 31. **Henry V** dies of dysentery at Vincennes. Thirty-five. He never wore the French crown.

October 21. **Charles VI** dies in Paris.

**Henry VI**, nine months old, is King of England and France.

In Bourges, the Dauphin proclaims himself **Charles VII**. The "King of Bourges." A joke.

### ACT V: THE MAID & THE TURN (1428–1435)

**[HEAD FAKE]**

*England is winning. Bedford, the Regent, is a genius of administration. The English hold Paris, Normandy, Maine, Anjou, Gascony. The Scots are crushed at Verneuil. The Dauphinist cause is a rump state south of the Loire.*

*Then a peasant girl arrives.*

**1429. Chinon.**

She wears men’s clothes. She says Saints Michael, Catherine, and Margaret sent her. The Dauphin hides among courtiers. She walks straight to him. *"God give you good life, gentle Dauphin."*

**[FUN FACT INTEGRATION]**

*She is seventeen. Illiterate. She has never held a sword. But she knows things she cannot know. The test was devised to expose a fraud. She passed it.*

**Orléans. The Gateway to the South.**

The English have ringed it with forts—*bastilles*. The Tourelles commands the bridge. The Earl of Salisbury is dead, killed by a cannonball at the window. Talbot commands now.

**April 29. She enters the city.** The English do nothing. Their aura of invincibility paralyzes them.

**May 4–7. The Assaults.** St. Loup. Les Augustins. The Tourelles. She is wounded by an arrow—pulls it out, returns. The English abandon the siege. **May 8. They burn their camps and leave.**

**June 18. Patay.**

Talbot tries to retreat. The French vanguard—La Hire, Xaintrailles, Dunois, Richemont—catches them in the open. The English archers haven't planted stakes. The French heavy cavalry hits them at full gallop.

**[FUN FACT INTEGRATION]**

*The stakes were left behind in the mud yesterday. The bowstrings are soaked by rain. The longbow—the weapon that won Crécy, Poitiers, Agincourt—is useless. The English field army is annihilated. Talbot captured. Fastolf flees—branded a coward.*

**July 17, 1429. Reims.**

Charles VII is anointed with the Holy Ampulla. The oil of Clovis. The Dauphin is King. The Treaty of Troyes is dead.

**1430. Compiègne.**

She is captured by Burgundians. Sold to the English. Ten thousand livres.

**1431. Rouen.**

The trial. Bishop Cauchon. The University of Paris. She is condemned not for her voices, but for *wearing men's clothes*—a relapse after she put them back on when guards stole her dress.

**[FUN FACT INTEGRATION]**

*A technicality. A stolen dress. She burns in the market square. Her heart, they say, does not burn. The English secretary weeps: "We have burned a saint."*

**1435. Arras.**

The Congress. The English refuse to renounce Henry VI’s crown. Philip the Good demands the Somme towns, an apology for Montereau, exemption from homage. Charles VII swallows his pride. He grants it all.

**Burgundy defects. Paris falls. The English position collapses.**

---

### ACT VI: THE GUN & THE STATE (1435–1453)

**[REHOOK]**

*The war changes. The longbow is obsolete. The knight is obsolete. The feudal host is obsolete.*

**Charles VII builds the first standing army since Rome.**

**1439. The Ordonnance.** Fifteen companies. One hundred lances each. Nine thousand professional cavalry. Paid by the permanent *taille*. Captains loyal to the Crown, not to their lineage.

**The Bureau Brothers. Jean and Gaspard.** Masters of Artillery. They standardize calibers. They corn the powder—granulated, powerful, reliable. They cast iron balls—cheaper than stone. They build wagons. They create the *artillery park*: a fortified camp of trenches, palisades, and guns.

**1444. Tours.** A truce. Henry VI marries Margaret of Anjou. Maine is surrendered—secretly.

**1449. The French break the truce.** They want Normandy.

**April 15, 1450. Formigny.**

An English army marches to relieve Bayeux. French guns deploy on the flanks. The longbows engage. The culverins shatter the formations. Breton cavalry hits the flank. Normandy is lost.

**1451. Gascony Falls.** Bordeaux. Bayonne. Three hundred years of English rule ends in months.

**1452. The Last Gamble.** Bordeaux revolts. They beg for Talbot. The old Earl—seventy, the "English Achilles"—lands with 3,000 men. He retakes the city.

**July 17, 1453. Castillon.**

Talbot marches to relieve the siege of Castillon. He sees a dust cloud—*the French are fleeing!* He charges.

**[FUN FACT INTEGRATION]**

*He has just finished breakfast. The dust is not fleeing Frenchmen. It is the wagons of the Bureau brothers' artillery park. Three hundred guns. In trenches. Behind palisades. The enfilade fire catches the English column in the open. Cannonballs take legs, heads, horses. Talbot’s horse is killed. He is pinned. A French archer finishes him with an axe.*

**October 19, 1453. Bordeaux surrenders.**

No treaty. No ceremony. The English simply sail home.

---

### EPILOGUE: THE INHERITANCE

**[CONSEQUENCES]**

**England.** Henry VI collapses into madness. The dukes of York and Somerset—bankrupt by the war, armed with retainers—turn their swords on each other. **The Wars of the Roses.** The Plantagenets destroy themselves. The Tudor rose blooms on a battlefield.

**France.** Charles VII—the "Victorious"—rules a unified kingdom. A standing army. Permanent taxation. A compliant Church (Pragmatic Sanction). The foundations of **Absolutism**. Louis XI waits in the wings.

**Burgundy.** Charles the Bold dreams of a kingdom between France and Germany. He dies at Nancy in 1477. His lands split: the Duchy to France, the Low Countries to the Habsburgs. The map of modern Europe takes shape.

**The Soldiers.** The longbowmen vanish. The *compagnies d'ordonnance* become the French gendarmerie. The artillery park becomes the siege train. The indenture system becomes the standing regiment.

**The Myth.** Shakespeare makes Henry V a hero, Joan a witch. The French Republic makes Joan a saint. The English remember Agincourt; the French remember Castillon. Both nations forged in the same fire.

---

### FINAL IMAGE

**Castillon-la-Bataille. Today.**

A field. A river. A small monument. Tourists picnic where the artillery park stood. The cannons are in museums—Paris, London, Leeds. The longbows rotted centuries ago; only the *Mary Rose* yields their ghosts.

The war lasted 116 years. Five generations. It began with a feudal technicality—homage for a duchy. It ended with the birth of the modern state.

*The King of England is no longer a vassal. The King of France answers to no one.*
