The year is 1793. The French Revolution has eaten its own children. The guillotine works overtime in Paris. The armies of Europe mass on the borders, intent on strangling the Republic in its cradle. And in the Mediterranean, a British fleet sits anchored off Toulon, handing the port — France’s great naval arsenal — to royalist traitors on a silver platter. If Toulon falls, the Republic loses its fleet, its southern flank collapses, and the revolution likely dies in its infancy.

Into this chaos steps a twenty-four-year-old artillery captain from Corsica. He has no noble blood, no powerful patrons, and a heavy accent that marks him as an outsider. But he sees what the generals cannot: that the key to the harbor is not the city walls, but a single, exposed hilltop fort called L’Aiguillette. He places his guns there. He fights alongside his men in the mud, taking a bayonet wound to the thigh. When the British finally evacuate, they burn their own fleet rather than let it fall into his hands.

Toulon saves the Revolution. It makes Napoleon Bonaparte a Brigadier General at an age when most men are still lieutenants. But the Revolution he saved is already devouring itself. Within a year, his patrons, the Robespierre brothers, are dead. Napoleon is arrested, suspected of treason, stripped of his command. He hits rock bottom in Paris — penniless, humiliated, reading mathematics books in a cold room, wondering if his moment has passed before it ever truly arrived.

Then comes the whisper of a new threat. A royalist mob marches on the Convention in October 1795. The government has no troops, no plan, only a desperate plea for a general willing to fire on Frenchmen. Napoleon arrives at the Church of Saint-Roch. He doesn't bring an army. He brings forty cannons. He loads them with grapeshot — anti-personnel murder in a can. He waits until the column is packed tight in the narrow streets. Then he gives the order. The "whiff of grapeshot" cuts the rebellion down in minutes. Four thousand rounds. One thousand dead. The Directory survives. Napoleon becomes the sword of the Republic.

But the Directory is rotting. Corrupt. Bankrupt. Dependent on the very generals it fears. The question hanging over Paris in 1799 is not *if* the government will fall, but *who* will catch it. Napoleon is in Egypt, chasing a dream of empire in the East, his fleet burned at the bottom of Aboukir Bay by a British admiral named Nelson. He abandons his army there — slips away on a fast frigate through the blockade — because he knows the real prize is in Paris.

The Coup of 18 Brumaire is a farce that becomes a dictatorship. He stumbles in the Council of Five Hundred, loses his nerve, nearly gets stabbed. His brother Lucien saves him by lying to the troops outside, claiming the deputies tried to assassinate Napoleon. The grenadiers clear the hall. By nightfall, the Directory is gone. The Consulate is born. Napoleon is First Consul. He is thirty years old.

He does not rest. He crosses the Alps in the snow — not on a rearing white charger as the propaganda painting later shows, but on a sure-footed mule, guided by a local peasant. He descends into Italy behind the Austrian lines. At Marengo, he nearly loses everything. His army is split. The Austrians smash his center. By noon, the battle is lost. He sends desperate messages to his missing corps commander, Desaix. "Come back. I need you." Desaix arrives with fresh troops, leads a counterattack, dies in the moment of victory. Napoleon wins by the skin of his teeth. But he writes the bulletin himself. He writes the legend. Marengo makes him untouchable.

Peace follows. The Treaty of Amiens. For fourteen months, Europe breathes. Napoleon uses the silence to build the state the Revolution promised but never delivered. He creates the Bank of France. He mints the Franc Germinal — sound money, gold and silver, stable for a century. He rewrites the law in the Civil Code: equality before the law, property rights sacred, religious freedom, the end of feudal privilege. But he writes the subjugation of women into the same articles. He signs the Concordat with the Pope, healing the Church schism, making Catholicism the "religion of the majority" while the state holds the leash. He creates the Prefects — the Emperor's hand in every village. He founds the Lycées and the Legion of Honor. "Every soldier carries a marshal's baton in his knapsack." Meritocracy, yes. But a meritocracy built for war.

In 1804, he crowns himself Emperor. In Notre Dame, the Pope sits waiting to anoint him. Napoleon takes the crown from the altar and places it on his own head. Then he crowns Josephine. "God gives it to me," he says, "woe to him who touches it." The Revolution is over. The Empire begins.

But the British are still at sea. He gathers the Army of England at Boulogne, two thousand ships, a hundred thousand men. He stares across the Channel. He needs just six hours of calm water. He will never get them. Nelson and the Royal Navy own the waves. At Trafalgar, the Franco-Spanish fleet is annihilated. The invasion is impossible. So Napoleon turns his back on the ocean and marches east.

What follows is the year of miracles. Ulm. He wheels his seven corps — the *bataillon-carré*, the square battalion — around an entire Austrian army. Sixty thousand men surrender without a major battle. He enters Vienna. He lures the Russians and Austrians onto the frozen Pratzen Heights at Austerlitz. He deliberately weakens his right flank. The Allies take the bait. They pour forward. The center is empty. The French surge up the heights. The sun breaks through the mist — the "Sun of Austerlitz." The Allied center shatters. Thousands drown in the frozen Satschan ponds as the ice gives way under French cannon fire. The Holy Roman Empire, a thousand years old, dissolves six months later. Napoleon reorganizes Germany into the Confederation of the Rhine. He is the master of the continent.

He thinks he can master the economy too. The Berlin Decree. The Continental System. A blockade of Britain by land. No British goods. No British ships. He thinks he can starve the island nation into submission. Instead, he starves his own allies. The ports of Hamburg and Amsterdam rot. Smuggling becomes an industry. The blockade requires him to police the coasts of Europe — which means he must control Spain.

It is the fatal error. He lures the Spanish Bourbons to Bayonne, forces them to abdicate, puts his brother Joseph on the throne. Madrid rises on the Second of May. The repression is brutal. Goya paints the horror. But the Spanish do not surrender. They invent a new word for war: *guerrilla*. Little war. It consumes three hundred thousand French troops. It bleeds the Empire white. Wellington lands in Portugal. The "Spanish Ulcer" never heals.

Meanwhile, the Tsar of Russia, Alexander, opens his ports to neutral ships. The blockade leaks. Napoleon assembles the largest army the world has ever seen. Six hundred thousand men. Poles, Germans, Italians, Swiss, Austrians, Prussians — a European army marching to enforce a French peace. He crosses the Niemen in June 1812. He expects a quick battle. The Russians refuse. They retreat. They burn their own crops. They burn Smolensk. They burn Moscow.

At Borodino, seventy thousand men fall in a single day. The bloodiest day in history until the Somme. Napoleon takes the Great Redoubt. He holds the field. But the Russian army limps away, intact. He enters Moscow, a city of ashes. He waits in the Kremlin for a peace offer that never comes. Five weeks. The snow falls. He turns for home.

The retreat is not a march. It is a dissolution. The horses die first — no shoes for ice, no fodder. The cavalry ceases to exist. At the Berezina, the river is not frozen. Two bridges built by pontonniers working waist-deep in freezing water, dying of hypothermia even as they hammer the bolts. The army crosses. The stragglers don't. The bridges are cut. Thousands crushed, drowned, captured. Of the six hundred thousand who crossed the Niemen, perhaps thirty thousand effective soldiers stagger back across the border. The Grande Armée is dead.

Europe sees the blood in the water. Prussia switches sides. Austria hesitates, then joins. Napoleon raises new armies — boys of seventeen, old men, conscripts called up years early. He wins at Lützen and Bautzen. His tactical genius is undimmed. But he has no cavalry to pursue. He agrees to an armistice. The Allies use the time to sign the Trachenberg Plan: *Do not fight Napoleon. Fight his marshals.* It works. His subordinates are beaten at Großbeeren, Katzbach, Kulm, Dennewitz.

Leipzig. The Battle of Nations. Three hundred sixty thousand Allies against one hundred ninety thousand French. Four days of slaughter. On the third day, a Saxon brigade defects mid-battle, turning their guns on the French. On the fourth day, a corporal panics and blows the only bridge over the Elster too early. Thirty thousand Frenchmen are trapped. Poniatowski, the Polish prince, drowns trying to swim the river. Napoleon rides west, alone in his carriage, the master of a continent that no longer exists.

The 1814 campaign in France is a masterpiece of a different kind. Seventy thousand men against half a million. He uses interior lines. He moves like a whip. Champaubert, Montmirail, Château-Thierry, Vauchamps. Six days. He destroys Blücher’s army piecemeal. It is the best he has ever fought. But the arithmetic does not lie. Paris falls. The marshals mutiny. Ney tells him: "The army will not march." He abdicates at Fontainebleau. He tries to poison himself. The dose is old. He only vomits. They give him Elba. A tiny island. A toy kingdom. He keeps his title. He keeps his guard. He watches the sea.

Ten months. He reads the newspapers. He sees the Bourbons alienating the army, the people, the peasants. He sees the Congress of Vienna squabbling over the map. He sees his chance. He lands at Golfe-Juan with a thousand men. "If there is a soldier among you who wants to kill his Emperor, here I am." The 5th Regiment at Grenoble lowers their muskets. "Long live the Emperor." He walks to Paris in twenty days. Louis XVIII flees. The Hundred Days begin.

The Allies declare him an outlaw. They mobilize seven hundred thousand men. Napoleon has perhaps two hundred thousand. He must knock out Wellington and Blücher in Belgium before the Russians and Austrians arrive. He almost does. At Ligny, he beats Blücher. But the Prussians retreat *north*, toward Wellington, not east toward Germany. Napoleon misses it. He sends Grouchy to pursue the Prussians. Grouchy follows orders literally, missing the strategic point. At Waterloo, the ground is soaked by rain. Napoleon waits for the mud to dry so his artillery can move. He loses hours.

Wellington sits on the ridge of Mont-Saint-Jean. His men lie in the tall grass behind the crest. Napoleon throws everything at Hougoumont, at La Haye Sainte, at the center. Ney leads cavalry charge after charge against British squares — nine thousand horses, forty attacks, no infantry support, no spike nails to disable the captured guns. The squares hold. The Prussians arrive on the French right. Plancenoit burns. The Young Guard dies holding the village.

At seven-thirty in the evening, Napoleon commits the Middle Guard. The last reserve. They advance up the slope. The British Guards lie down. "Up, Guards, and at them!" The volley shatters the first rankes the column. The Guard recoils. "La Garde recule!" The cry spreads like a virus. The army dissolves. Napoleon rides away. He abdicates again. He surrenders to the British on the *Bellerophon*, hoping for a quiet retirement in England. They send him to St. Helena. A rock in the South Atlantic. Damp. Windy. Rat-infested.

He lives six years. He dictates his memoir to Las Cases. He rewrites the story. He becomes the Prometheus of the Revolution, the champion of the people, the victim of British gold. He dies of stomach cancer — or arsenic, the debate never ends — on May 5, 1821. His last words: "France, l'armée, tête d'armée, Joséphine."

Nineteen years later, his body returns to Paris. A million people line the streets. The July Monarchy, nervous of his ghost, gives him a state funeral. He rests now under the dome of the Invalides, the soldier's church, the giant sarcophagus of red quartzite on a green granite base.

But the tomb is not the legacy. The Code Civil governs seventy nations today. The metric system measures the world. The corps system structures every modern army. The map of Europe — Germany unified, Italy unified, the end of the Holy Roman Empire — was drawn by his conquests. He destroyed the old world. He built the skeleton of the new one. He was the Revolution on horseback. He was the Revolution's executioner. He was the man who held the world in his hand, and watched it crumble because he could not stop reaching for more.
